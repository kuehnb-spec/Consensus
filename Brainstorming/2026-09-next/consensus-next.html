Item 1

The new NVIDIA model

"There is a new Nvidia diarization model out — let's see if we can use it."

Nemotron 3 Diarization shipped today: 100M parameters, up to 8 speakers (the older Sortformer line capped at 4), streaming and offline in one checkpoint, and the OpenMDW 1.1 license, which allows commercial use. NVIDIA reports 12.7% DER offline and #1 on VoiceArena's Diarization-Bench. FluidAudio, the Swift library we already depend on, added CoreML support in v0.17.0 this afternoon, and FluidInference/nemotron-3-diarization-coreml is on Hugging Face.

I built FluidAudio 0.17.1 and ran Nemotron on every clip in the new corpus on the Studio's Neural Engine: about 24 seconds for 10 minutes of audio, fully on-device. I ran the same audio through three comparisons: pyannote community-1 (FluidAudio's best offline pipeline), Parakeet v3 words assigned to each diarizer's speakers, and our current VibeVoice CLI.

cpWER counts a word wrong if the text is wrong or it's credited to the wrong speaker, so it's the number that matches your experience of a bad transcript. Lower is better. Across 15 Supreme Court clips, 149 minutes, 6–10 speakers each. Word error is content WER: fillers and stutters are ignored on both sides.

ConfigurationWord errorcpWERSpeaker confusionDER

DER = missed speech + false alarm + wrong speaker, 0.25 s collar. The raw diarizers show higher "missed" time because Oyez turns include the short pauses between sentences; speaker confusion is the fair comparison between them.

Swapping in Nemotron as the speaker engine would make transcripts worse on average: 8.2% → 12.4% cpWER with the same words. It undercounts speakers (6 of 10 on Google v. Oracle, where VibeVoice found all 10), and the 8-speaker cap bites on a nine-justice bench. But the per-clip table shows the two fail in different places. Nemotron was better on 5 of 15 clips, and on Weyerhaeuser it cut DER from 10.3% to 1.3% where VibeVoice's labels drifted. Two witnesses that fail differently is exactly what Consensus is built to use.

Verdict: adopt it, but as a witness, not the judge. Nemotron earns a place three ways: (1) a second speaker opinion, since disagreement between two diarizers is exactly where attribution errors hide; (2) the fallback when VibeVoice can't run, for audio over 60 minutes or on a smaller Mac, where it beat community-1 clearly; (3) a future live mode, since it streams at 1 s latency.

Update, Sept 25: Nemotron as a discrepancy witness

You asked the right question: even if it loses head-to-head, does adding Nemotron to the stack, flagging where it disagrees and resolving those spots, lower cpWER? I measured it word by word.

VibeVoice speakers, then…cpWER
nothing (today)8.18%
every Nemotron / community-1 flag resolved perfectly (the ceiling)7.45%
voice match on the exact disputed audio (CAM++, strictest margin)8.28%
2-of-3 vote with community-1 as tiebreaker10.48%

The theory holds, with a caveat. Nemotron's flags do find real errors: it disagrees on 4.6% of words and catches 37% of VibeVoice's wrong-speaker words, so perfect resolution would cut cpWER by about 9%. But on this corpus it's right in only 8–13% of its disputes. That's roughly nine false alarms per real catch, because it merges similar voices. The two acoustic diarizers share that failure, so a vote between them overrules VibeVoice when it was right (413 words broken, 59 fixed). And half of VibeVoice's errors are whole sentences credited to the wrong justice, which Nemotron usually gets wrong the same way.

So "unless it makes the same mistakes" is the crux, and here it partly does. Six to ten similar voices is Nemotron's worst case, though. The next test is 2–3-speaker audio like your calls: your gold files, once they're copied to the Studio, or the AMI meetings. Two resolvers are still untested: Nemotron's own frame confidence, and a judge that reads the disputed lines for conversational sense ("Justice Kavanaugh? Justice Barrett?" is a Chief Justice line).

Integration cost is modest: move from the vendored FluidAudio snapshot (years of releases behind) to a pinned remote 0.17.1. The wip/studio branch already has half the API migration. Re-validate the CLI afterward, per the July handoff guardrails.

Item 2

The interface

"Keep the multi-mode setup, make it more beautiful and unique, and simplify files and projects."

What's making it feel clunky

  • Two storage systems. The app keeps projects as UUID folders hidden in Application Support; the CLI writes .consensus.json next to the audio. A recording your PADD pipeline transcribed overnight never shows up in the app.
  • Links that break. Projects soft-link the original audio, so moving or renaming the file breaks the project, and there's no relink.
  • No way to group. Projects are one flat list with no matter, client, or folder.
  • The pipeline is the UI. The main view is a stage router that swaps the entire screen eight times, plus six modal sheets. You pick a mode before you've seen anything, and passes, tiers, and engines leak into the vocabulary.

Files: one recording, one place, one format

  • A library with Matters. The sidebar holds Inbox (new arrivals, including everything the PADD watcher transcribes), Matters (your folders), All recordings, and Voices. Drop audio anywhere and it lands in the Inbox and starts.
  • Audio is copied in, once. It's addressed by content hash, so re-importing the same file finds the existing recording. Moving or deleting the original never breaks anything.
  • Each recording is a readable package: Harbor call.consensus/ holding the audio, transcript.json (the CLI's schema 2.0, extended), history/, and notes.md. The CLI writes the same package, so the app and automation become two doors into one room. Drag a package out to hand a matter to co-counsel.
  • Passes become history. You see one transcript with a status: Draft → Checked → Verified → Certified. Earlier versions live in a timeline, like document versions. Export is an action, not a stage.
  • The library lives on local disk, never iCloud (the August lesson), and can be relocated.

Modes: lenses on the same recording, not three forks

Keep the three modes, but decouple how you look from how hard the machine works. The mode is a lens you can switch mid-read; accuracy is a separate dial you can raise later ("Make this court-ready" runs the heavier pipeline in the background and upgrades the same recording).

ReadQuick Take becomes a clean typeset transcript with speaker names and copy/export. No marks and no machinery.
ReviewDeep Read becomes a highlighter view. Disputed spans are marked, and a queue sorted by risk tells you "7 spans, about 4 minutes." Click, listen, accept.
StudioThe instrument view: engine channel strips, the full evidence trace, and the convergence strip in detail.

Identity: the transcript as a legal record

The July design memo diagnosed the current look as AI-generated: a Tailwind palette, glassmorphism, and chip rows. I agree, and I'd push its fix further into your world. The document is typeset like a certified transcript: page:line numbering in the gutter, speaker names in small caps with a colon, and a Cite 14:7 copy action, because that's how you cite a deposition. Disputes aren't red alarms; they're highlighter, the way you mark a transcript by hand, and the highlight disappears once the span is settled. Above the page, the convergence strip draws each engine as a thread: where the engines agree they run as one line of ink, where they disagree they splay apart. Consensus being forged, literally drawn. The paper can be day (bond white) or night (umber), and the desk around it stays dark.

Item 2b

Motion that reports what's happening

"Can we get fancy, and add a couple of interesting motion design flourishes… to indicate when things are happening and what they are?"

The rule: every motion reports a real event, so you can tell what the machine is doing without reading a label. One signature move carries the identity, the convergence of the engines' threads, and everything else stays quick and quiet. Nothing pulses, breathes, or shimmers for decoration. Threads converge signatureEach engine draws its own thread across the strip as it listens; its pen tip is its real progress. Where engines hear the same words, the threads run as one line. Where they disagree, the threads splay apart, and when the disagreement is settled they spring back together. You watch consensus form.
Wet inkWords arrive warm and slightly soft, then dry to ink within a second. Wet means the engine may still revise it; dry means committed. A word that later gets corrected goes wet again, so you see exactly what changed.
Highlighter swipeWhen the witnesses disagree about a span, a highlighter stroke sweeps under it, the way you'd mark a transcript by hand. Settling it wipes the highlight away left to right and leaves a faint dotted underline as the audit trail.
The loupeWhen the verifier re-listens to a span, the word is masked to a blank, a small waveform loupe scans that audio, and the answer is typed into the blank. It's the masked-cloze check, made visible.
Speaker lanesBeneath the threads, one lane per voice grows as the diarizer hears it. A new speaker opens a new lane, and when you name someone the name sets into the lane in small caps.
Line counter and sealThe page:line counter ticks forward like a court reporter's, so the status line always shows a citable position. When the last dispute is settled, a seal presses into the status line once: Verified · 0 open.

With macOS Reduce Motion on, every motion becomes an instant state change carrying the same information. In SwiftUI: the strip is a Canvas inside a TimelineView driven by real engine progress; the ink, highlighter, and seal are KeyframeAnimators; springs settle in under 400 ms.

Item 3

Test subjects without the editing

"Publicly available transcripts of movies and speeches… the sheer volume of consistent transcripts lets us be confident."

Your instinct, that independent agreement earns trust, is exactly right, and it's the core of what I built. Movies are the wrong material for it, though. The audio is copyrighted, fan transcripts are cleaned up and not time-aligned, score and effects sit under the dialogue, and acted speech doesn't interrupt the way a deposition does. We'd end up measuring how transcribers edit rather than how well the engines hear.

Better: the U.S. Supreme Court. The audio is a federal record in the public domain. The words come from the Court's official reporter. Oyez has aligned every turn to the audio with the speaker's name. That's legal register, 6–10 speakers per clip, and the same nine voices recurring across hundreds of recordings, which is a free test for the Voice Library. And the 2020 term was argued entirely by telephone.

How the reference gets checked without you editing it

Each clip is run through two engines built by different teams on different data. Where both engines agree with each other and not with the reference, the reference is probably the odd one out, and that turn goes on a listen-list with a three-second audio snippet. Everything else is trusted. On the first pass, the list surfaced something important: the official transcript is clean verbatim. The reporter drops stutters and false starts, so "there there there is" becomes "there's." The scorer now collapses repeated words on both sides, which cut the first seven clips' list from 29 turns to 19. Across all 15 clips it's 29 of 1,176 turns (2.5%), each with a snippet. Most are timing drift in Oyez's 1990s alignments. That's about five minutes of listening, and you only do it once per clip.

Item 4

Reopening the accuracy question

"Once we got it working reasonably well we stopped testing alternative ideas — maybe it's time."

Tonight's numbers already overturn one assumption: the July plan treated VibeVoice's speaker tags as an unused second opinion, but they're the best diarization we have. VibeVoice also leads on words: 5.5% content WER against Parakeet's 7.6%. But Parakeet is 40× faster, and its errors land in different places ("ripeness" versus VibeVoice's "rightness"), which is what a consensus design needs.

One flaw surfaced along the way: VibeVoice has no real word-level confidence. Every word the CLI emits carries 0.95, the segment's score copied down. That quietly breaks the handoff's "never fabricate confidence" rule, and it means confidence can't be used to rank spans for review until we get it from a second engine. It's a small, contained CLI fix: emit null at word level.

One number decides whether the current patch-review architecture has headroom: what share of real errors fall inside spans where the engines disagree? Patch review can only fix what's flagged. If 80% of errors are disputed, invest in the verifier. If 40%, errors that every engine shares dominate, and a plausibility pass matters more. Now that there's a corpus with ground truth, that's measurable.

  1. A

    Measure disagreement recall first

    For every true error, check whether it sits inside a disputed span (two witnesses, then three). This tells us where the remaining accuracy lives before we spend a week on any engine.

    1 day · no downloads
  2. B

    Speaker consensus

    Fuse VibeVoice, Nemotron, and community-1 speaker turns with DOVER-Lap-style voting, and flag words where the diarizers disagree. The app's own thesis, applied to who said it.

    2 days · on hand
  3. C

    A third word witness

    Add Whisper large-v3 (already in the app) or Qwen3-ASR as a third voter, and do word-level ROVER alignment as the disagreement map. Three witnesses let a majority settle ties without a verifier.

    2 days · ~3 GB
  4. D

    Known roster and your voice as an anchor

    You're in most of your recordings. Enroll your voice once and pass the expected head-count ("me, opposing counsel, witness"). That's the cheapest large diarization win for structured calls. The Supreme Court corpus tests it, because the same justices recur.

    3 days · on hand
  5. E

    Plausibility pass for shared errors

    A text-only local LLM flags spans that don't make sense in context ("meditation" in an arbitration). Each flag becomes a masked re-listen, never a direct edit, per the Phase A lesson.

    3 days · 8–13 GB
  6. F

    Verifier bake-off, resumed

    Qwen3-Omni-30B versus Gemma 4 12B in the masked-cloze role, gated on zero edits to clean input. This needs disk space freed first.

    4 days · ~33 GB

Every one of these runs through the same three scripts and posts a row to the same table, so an idea is tested in hours, not argued about in memos.
